# People read intentions into moving shapes

**Study.** Heider, F., & Simmel, M. (1944). An experimental study of apparent behavior. *The American Journal of Psychology, 57*(2), 243–259. [doi:10.2307/1416950](https://doi.org/10.2307/1416950)

**In the talk.** Part 2, [A group: people as the answer](../../docs/part2-people-as-the-answer.md): people read intentions into moving shapes.

**Question.** When people watch simple geometric figures move, do they see movements, or actions with purposes?

**Method.** A film of about two and a half minutes shows three geometric figures (a large triangle, a small triangle and a circle) moving in and around a rectangle with a door. Three groups of undergraduate women each watched it twice:
- **Group I (34 participants)** received only general instructions: "write down what happened in the picture";
- **Group II (36 participants)** were asked to interpret the movements as actions of persons and to answer a set of questions;
- **Group III (44 participants)** saw the film in reverse.

**Finding.** All but one participant in Group I, all in Group II and all but two in Group III described the film as actions of animated beings, mostly persons. Some readings were almost uniform. For example, every participant saw the figures pushing or pulling the door, whether the film ran forwards or in reverse. Readings of motives and causes varied much more, especially for the reversed film. The authors take this as a sign of "more projection".

**Why read it.** It is the classic demonstration that people do not just register what they see; they add what they infer. It also shows where observers agree, on actions, and where they diverge, on meanings. That is the distinction this part asks you to keep when a model describes or rates people.

**Limits.** The participants were 114 undergraduate women in the 1940s. The study reports descriptions and their themes, not a modern agreement statistic.

**Data and code.** The 1944 article is on JSTOR; no data set was released.
