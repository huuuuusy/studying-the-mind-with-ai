# A reading path, step by step

Three steps, from an afternoon's reading to the open debates. Each step lists a few readings
with one line on why to read them; the [full lists](#the-full-lists) are at the end.

## Step 1 · Start here (an afternoon)

Four readings that together give the whole picture: an old trick, an old test, and two maps
of the present.

1. Pfungst, O. (1911). *Clever Hans (the horse of Mr. von Osten).* [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33936). A horse that seemed to count was reading tiny, unintended movements of its questioner's head: the first control experiment on a "too clever" answer.
2. Turing, A. M. (1950). Computing machinery and intelligence. *Mind.* [doi:10.1093/mind/lix.236.433](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/lix.236.433). The imitation game: judging a machine by whether people can tell it apart.
3. Demszky, D., et al. (2023). Using large language models in psychology. *Nature Reviews Psychology.* [doi:10.1038/s44159-023-00241-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44159-023-00241-5). A broad map of what language models can and cannot do for psychology.
4. Geirhos, R., et al. (2020). Shortcut learning in deep neural networks. *Nature Machine Intelligence.* [doi:10.1038/s42256-020-00257-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-020-00257-z). Why a right answer can come from the wrong cue.

## Step 2 · One part at a time

Read the key readings of each part in this order. Each links to a one-page card with the
question, method, finding, limits and data.

### Part 1 · One person: people next to the machine

1. [Geirhos et al., 2019](examples/geirhos2019-texture-bias.md): one cue against another, people and networks alike.
2. [Geirhos, Meding & Wichmann, 2020](examples/geirhos2020-error-consistency.md): from which cue wins to which items fail.
3. [Lapuschkin et al., 2019](examples/lapuschkin2019-clever-hans.md): the Clever Hans test applied to a classifier (a model that sorts images into categories).
4. [Geirhos et al., 2021](examples/geirhos2021-partial-success.md): accuracy and consistency can tell different stories.
5. [Fu et al., 2024](examples/fu2024-blink.md): the human baseline for multimodal models (models that take in images as well as text).

How they connect: [Part 1, *How they connect*](../docs/part1-people-next-to-machine.md#how-they-connect).

### Part 2 · A group: people as the answer

1. [Heider & Simmel, 1944](examples/heider1944-moving-shapes.md): what people see and what they read into it.
2. [Ouyang et al., 2022](examples/ouyang2022-human-feedback.md): people's comparisons as the reference behind chat models.
3. [Peterson et al., 2019](examples/peterson2019-cifar10h.md): a full distribution of human labels, used for training.
4. [Morgenroth et al., 2025](examples/morgenroth2025-emo-film.md): a public human reference that disagrees.
5. [Uma et al., 2021](examples/uma2021-learning-from-disagreement.md): the field that learns from disagreement.

How they connect: [Part 2, *How they connect*](../docs/part2-people-as-the-answer.md#how-they-connect).

### Part 3 · A society: people we make

1. [Park et al., 2022](examples/park2022-social-simulacra.md): where Smallville came from: many different test users, on demand.
2. [Park et al., 2023](examples/park2023-generative-agents.md): simulated people that look believable.
3. [Park et al., 2024](examples/park2024-simulating-1000-people.md): a simulated person judged against the same person, measured twice.
4. [Argyle et al., 2023](examples/argyle2023-silicon-samples.md): a model answering in place of survey respondents.
5. [Serapio-García et al., 2025](examples/serapio2025-personality.md): a persona measured with psychometric standards.

How they connect: [Part 3, *How they connect*](../docs/part3-people-we-make.md#how-they-connect).

### Part 4 · AI in your own research

1. [Demszky et al., 2023](examples/demszky2023-llms-in-psychology.md): the map, before any project.
2. [Gilardi et al., 2023, with Reiss, 2023](examples/gilardi2023-ai-annotation.md): a model as a coder, its promise and its instability.
3. [Rathje et al., 2024](examples/rathje2024-psychological-text.md): measuring psychological constructs in text.
4. [Angelopoulos et al., 2023](examples/angelopoulos2023-prediction-powered-inference.md): valid conclusions from model labels.
5. [Ashokkumar et al., 2026](examples/ashokkumar2026-predicting-experiments.md): simulated participants as a pretest.

How they connect: [Part 4, *How they connect*](../docs/part4-ai-in-your-research.md#how-they-connect).

## Step 3 · Frontier and debates

Readings that push back, or open the next question.

### Part 1

- Binz, M., & Schulz, E. (2023). Using cognitive psychology to understand GPT-3. *PNAS.* [doi:10.1073/pnas.2218523120](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218523120). Classic cognitive-psychology tasks given to a language model.
- Ullman, T. (2023). Large language models fail on trivial alterations to theory-of-mind tasks. [arXiv:2302.08399](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08399). A small change to a task can undo an impressive result.
- McCoy, R. T., et al. (2024). Embers of autoregression show how large language models are shaped by the problem they are trained to solve. *PNAS.* [doi:10.1073/pnas.2322420121](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2322420121). Performance tracks what the model was trained to do, not only the task.
- Guest, O., & Martin, A. E. (2023). On logical inference over brains, behaviour, and artificial neural networks. *Computational Brain & Behavior.* [doi:10.1007/s42113-022-00166-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42113-022-00166-x). What a match between a model and human data does and does not let you conclude.
- Linsley, D., et al. (2023). Performance-optimized deep neural networks are evolving into worse models of inferotemporal visual cortex. *NeurIPS 2023.* [doi:10.52202/075280-1257](https://doi.org/10.52202/075280-1257). Better benchmark scores need not mean a better model of the brain.

### Part 2

- Aroyo, L., & Welty, C. (2015). Truth is a lie: Crowd truth and the seven myths of human annotation. *AI Magazine.* [doi:10.1609/aimag.v36i1.2564](https://doi.org/10.1609/aimag.v36i1.2564). Why there is often no single true label to recover.
- Plank, B. (2022). The "problem" of human label variation: On ground truth in data, modeling and evaluation. *EMNLP 2022.* [doi:10.18653/v1/2022.emnlp-main.731](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2022.emnlp-main.731). Disagreement as a property of the task, not only of the raters.
- Cabitza, F., et al. (2023). Toward a perspectivist turn in ground truthing for predictive computing. *AAAI 2023.* [doi:10.1609/aaai.v37i6.25840](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v37i6.25840). Keeping the perspectives of different groups of raters.
- Zheng, L., et al. (2023). Judging LLM-as-a-judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena. *NeurIPS 2023.* [doi:10.52202/075280-2020](https://doi.org/10.52202/075280-2020). A model as the judge: high agreement, and its biases.
- Barrett, L. F., et al. (2019). Emotional expressions reconsidered: Challenges to inferring emotion from human facial movements. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest.* [doi:10.1177/1529100619832930](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100619832930). Why a face is not a feeling: the limit on any machine that reads emotion.

### Part 3

- Bisbee, J., et al. (2024). Synthetic replacements for human survey data? The perils of large language models. *Political Analysis.* [doi:10.1017/pan.2024.5](https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2024.5). Where silicon samples mislead.
- Wang, A., et al. (2025). Large language models that replace human participants can harmfully misportray and flatten identity groups. *Nature Machine Intelligence.* [doi:10.1038/s42256-025-00986-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-025-00986-z). What is lost when a model speaks for a group.
- Han, P., et al. (2025). The personality illusion: Revealing dissociation between self-reports & behavior in LLMs. [arXiv:2509.03730](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.03730). A model's questionnaire answers and its behaviour can come apart.
- Akata, E., et al. (2025). Playing repeated games with large language models. *Nature Human Behaviour.* [doi:10.1038/s41562-025-02172-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02172-y). Behavioural game theory as a test of social behaviour in models.
- Schröder, S., et al. (2025). Large language models do not simulate human psychology. [arXiv:2508.06950](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06950). The case against simulated participants.

### Part 4

- Dillion, D., et al. (2023). Can AI language models replace human participants? *Trends in Cognitive Sciences.* [doi:10.1016/j.tics.2023.04.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2023.04.008). The question stated, with caveats.
- Pangakis, N., & Wolken, S. (2025). Keeping humans in the loop: Human-centered automated annotation with generative AI. *Proceedings of the International AAAI Conference on Web and Social Media (ICWSM).* [doi:10.1609/icwsm.v19i1.35883](https://doi.org/10.1609/icwsm.v19i1.35883). Validate against people, every time.
- Baumann, J., et al. (2025). Large language model hacking: Quantifying the hidden risks of using LLMs for text annotation. [arXiv:2509.08825](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2509.08825). How choices of model and prompt can change conclusions.
- Gao, Y., et al. (2025). Take caution in using LLMs as human surrogates. *PNAS.* [doi:10.1073/pnas.2501660122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2501660122). Why a simulation is not a sample.
- Crockett, M. J., & Messeri, L. (2026). AI surrogates and illusions of generalizability in cognitive science. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences.* [doi:10.1016/j.tics.2025.09.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2025.09.012). What simulated participants can make us believe too easily.

## Two ways through

- **Students.** Start from your own area of psychology in the [list for psychology students](students.md), then take Step 2 for the part closest to it, and read the [guide to working together](working-together.md).
- **Teachers.** Step 1 makes a first seminar; each part's key readings make one session; the [guide to working together](working-together.md) can open a discussion of how the two fields work together; the [handout](../docs/handout/handout.pdf) can be printed for a class.
